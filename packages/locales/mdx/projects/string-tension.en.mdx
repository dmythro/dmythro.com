I play a multi-scale eight-string guitar, and every time I ordered strings for it I was guessing. The scale length is different under each string, so a set that feels right on the low E often turns to piano wire up at the D. The tension calculators I found didn't help much. Some were missing an adjustment I needed or knew only one brand, and others forgot my inputs as soon as I left the page.

No signup, no installation: [open the calculator](/en/guitars/string-tension) and start from a preset.

The app lets you name two scale lengths and works the rest out semi-automatically. Usually you don't even do that much, as long as there is a preset for something close to your instrument. Everything is easy to bend to your own setup from there: scale, note and gauge are all per string. And every row is marked against a typical "normal".

## Examples

Each row is one call to the same formula the published tension tables are built on:

```ts
// tension in lbs, from gauge, scale length in inches, and pitch
calculateTension(".046w", 25.5, "E2"); // 18.1 — a low E at Strat scale
calculateTension(".046w", 27, "E2"); // 20.3 — the same string on a baritone neck
calculateTension(".062w", 27, "B1"); // 20.7 — B standard on that same neck, matched to the E above
```

Multi-scale needs one extra input: give the first and last strings a scale length, and the rest are interpolated. The frets fan out along straight lines, so the scale length does change evenly from one string to the next:

```ts
interpolateScale(0, 8, 26.5, 28); // 26.5   — high D
interpolateScale(4, 8, 26.5, 28); // 27.357
interpolateScale(7, 8, 26.5, 28); // 28     — low E
```

## Why it exists

A six-string in D standard, a seven-string and a multi-scale eight-string need different gauges to feel like the same instrument in the hand, or at least close to it. To work that out I needed a manufacturer's PDF, a unit-weight column, a calculator app and a row of arithmetic per string, and then all of it again whenever I changed my mind about the tuning. The web calculators that could have saved me the arithmetic stopped at six strings and one scale length, which left out the seven-string and the eight-string.

That's why the instrument presets are my own guitars. I wanted numbers for the instruments I play, not only for a typical Stratocaster in E.

## What's inside

### Published unit weights where they exist

Tension depends mostly on unit weight, the weight of the string per inch, and for a wound string you can't get that from the diameter. A .046 is a steel core with a wrap around it, and the ratio between the two is up to the manufacturer; standard geometry won't give it to you. So for D'Addario and Ernie Ball gauges, the calculator reads unit weight straight from their published tables.

### A fitted model where they don't

Everything else (Elixir sets, bass gauges, anything custom) falls back to `UW = diameter² × coefficient`. I fitted the coefficients to D'Addario's table: run a gauge that _is_ published there (any plain gauge, or a wound one from .024w up) through the formula instead of the lookup, and the two results are within 1% of each other. Against Ernie Ball's table the same formula stays within 2%. A test checks both. Beyond the end of the tables those margins are an extrapolation rather than a measurement, so read every bass number here with that caveat. The coefficients I used before were 15–22% too high, and nothing checked them.

### A reference set for comparison

Each row shows how far it is from a reference set: D'Addario 10-46 in E standard at 25.5" for guitar. Green means it should feel like a typical guitar, not that it's correct. Plenty of setups sit 20% away from it on purpose, so the arrow only shows which direction you've gone from the reference.

## Design notes

The calculator works in pounds and inches. The 386.4 in the formula's denominator is gravity in inches per second squared. Published tension tables for guitar strings are in pounds, so working in newtons would mean converting the source data, showing a number no string manufacturer prints, and adding a rounding step for no benefit.

The square law is only an approximation. For plain steel, unit weight does follow the diameter squared: the fitted coefficient comes out to π/4 × 0.282 lbs/in³, which is simply the density of music wire, and every plain gauge in the table agrees within 0.3%. Wound strings follow it only roughly. Below .024w the core makes up a large enough share of the diameter that the fit breaks down, and a .017w is off by 6%. Those six gauges are all in the lookup tables anyway, so instead of adding a second model I fitted the fallback to the range that actually reaches it, which is mostly bass.

The calculator saves your inputs in localStorage, since your instrument doesn't change between visits: gauges, notes and scale lengths come back as you left them. Tensions aren't saved. They're recomputed on every load, so a correction to the model also reaches setups saved before it, and after the coefficient fix described above, that mattered.

## Status

It's live and does the job I built it for. I add a preset when I get an instrument the existing ones don't describe.

The weak spot is bass. Every bass gauge goes through the fitted model, because the brand tables here cover guitar only. The bass numbers are published; I just haven't typed them in. This is the part of the tool I trust least, and the next thing I'd fix.
